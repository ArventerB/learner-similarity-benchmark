Retrieval-augmented knowledge tracing, cold-start matching and adaptive tutoring agents all retrieve learners who are "similar" to a given learner, yet the similarity measure is chosen without comparison: cosine in some systems, hand-weighted mixtures or a Mahalanobis distance in others. It is unknown which measure works when ability vectors are correlated and sparse and only a small cohort is available, or how the answer changes with the number of learners. We benchmark {{n_measures}} similarity measures (Euclidean, Manhattan, cosine and Pearson; Mahalanobis distances with sample and shrinkage covariance; PCA variants; and seven learned metrics including LMNN, NCA, ITML and MLKR) and three non-metric models on {{n_views}} learner views built from eight public sources, across four prediction tasks, feature dimensions of 20 to 100 and cohorts of 50 to 500 learners, with learner-disjoint evaluation and {{reps_min}} repetitions in each of {{n_cells}} cells. The cosine similarity on standardised vectors is the best or co-best retrieval measure at every cohort size (mean R² {{mean_r2/cosine/50|.2f}} to {{mean_r2/cosine/500|.2f}}, against {{mean_r2/euclid/50|.2f}} to {{mean_r2/euclid/500|.2f}} for Euclidean). Every form of whitening falls to the level of predicting the mean (Ledoit–Wolf Mahalanobis minus Euclidean: {{contrasts/maha_lw-euclid@all/diff|.2f}} R², in all eight sources), and the loss grows with the cohort. Learned metrics reach cosine at 500 learners and do not exceed it. A diagnostic that we call the signal–variance alignment explains the pattern: in every cell the outcome is carried by the high-variance directions that whitening suppresses (rank correlation with the whitening loss {{mechanism/d_maha~sva/0|.2f}}). No selection rule learned on other sources beats always using cosine; selection inside the cohort helps only from about 200 learners. A random instead of temporal outcome split inflates R² by about {{random_split/rel_inflation_ridge|.0%}}. We release the pipeline, code and a datasheet.
